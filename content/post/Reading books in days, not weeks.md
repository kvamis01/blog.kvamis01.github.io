I love reading a book that’s so good you start and finish it in a day or two.

I stayed up late on a lake vacation reading Peter Heller’s “The River” because I had to know whether the two friends would safely escape a wildfire on their wilderness canoe trip even as they paddled closer to the fire to investigate a missing person. 

I _technically_ read Agatha Christie’s “And Then There Were None” in one go, because when I fell asleep only three-quarters of the way through it, I woke up the next morning and immediately finished it before getting out of bed. There was a full 8 hours of sleep between the start and end, but it’s one go if I didn’t get out of bed!

It’s not just that good books compel us to read quickly. I think reading a book more quickly makes it more enjoyable. And not only because the plot progresses, but because our focus on the story is more attuned to its development.

Take my most recent read. I’ve been busy with work and chores, so each night when I laid down to read Lily King’s “Writers & Lovers” before bed, I could only make it a few pages at a time. I’d read about her walking her landlord’s dog, then fall asleep. Days later, I’d get to her dead-end waitressing job, then fall asleep again. I felt like I was watching a movie in five-minute increments, erasing any hope of an immersive reading experience.

This Saturday morning I restarted the book while deer hunting (which for me is really just drinking coffee and reading in camouflage clothing). Surprise, surprise, I couldn’t put it down. After an hour’s progress, I was invested in the protagonist working on her novel, cheering against dating the off-and-on, passionate writer for the older, more stable one — only to reverse course chapters later, again and again. By Sunday evening, I’d finished it.

I think there’s more going on than compelling storylines and character development, or finding a goldilocks reading pace, though.

When you read a book in a few days, you’re usually reading it in a headspace that lets you get more immersed in the story. When I’m reading too slowly it’s usually because my brain’s focusing on things to do, things I forgot about, chores, work, appointments. And when I speed-read, I’m usually more focused on _finishing_ the book than actually immersing myself and enjoying it.

But somewhere in the middle is the right headspace where I’m focused enough on the story to really enjoy it — but also giving myself enough reading stints to do other things and let my subconscious digest the story, too.

Each time we pick up a book, we’re a slightly different person. We’ve had new experiences, have new flavors of mental chatter, new problems our brains are working out in the background while we read. And each of these different versions of ourself color how we experience a book. So it’s not just that our reading pace affects how immersive or enjoyable a book is to us, but how many times we pick it up, too.

Maybe it’s not as complicated as I’m making it out to be. Maybe my favorite reading experiences are the good books I read in one go because they were so immersive I couldn’t stop turning pages. And while I know in the coming months I’ll have books I plow through and ones that drag on and on, I can’t help but hope each one will pull me out of my day-to-day and say “Here, lose yourself in this story for the next few days…”
