The Ray Bradbury challenge

November 3, 2026

Ray Bradbury has advice for beginning and intermediate writers: stuff your head full of good ideas by reading one poem, one short story, and one essay every night before bed. Then write one short story a week. Do this for a year, two years, 1,000 days — and you’ll be on your way.

There’s a lot that I love about this advice, which has been dubbed the Ray Bradbury Challenge online.

First, it’s about reading work that’s actually good. Books are made out of books, says Cormac McCarthy. You can’t expect to be a good writer if you’re not reading good writers! I spend too much of time scrolling videos and refreshing news headlines, the information equivalent of eating candy. But how much more nourishing would it be to fill my brain with good stories, interesting ideas, and beautiful writing instead?

Second, Bradbury’s advice teaches aspiring writers how to start a writing practice, which is about focusing on process before results. Instead of wasting your time overthinking how to write the perfect story, or spending a year wrestling with a bad idea for a novel, Bradbury’s advice is to focus on quantity over quality: write a short story one week. Finish it. Do it again next week. Many will be bad, but some are bound to be good. And 52 short stories in a year is something to be very proud of!

The book “Art & Fear” shares a similar lesson: A ceramics teacher tells half his students they’ll be graded on the quantity of pots they can make in a semester and the other half graded on quality. The former simply has to make fifty pounds of pots for an A, the latter has to produce just one pot for an A — but it had to be perfect. At the end of the semester, the curious thing that happened was the highest-quality pots were all made by the students graded on quantity, not quality. These students spent the semester churning out work and learning from their mistakes, while the “quality” students spent their theorizing about quality.

The lesson: Quantity leads to quality. Spend your time doing and learning, not thinking and theorizing. When I catch myself feeling like I should be writing more, I usually tell myself to spend less time thinking about writing and more time thinking while writing.

Bradbury’s practice is about leaning into quantity. When you’re a beginning writer, you don’t know what works well yet. The important thing is to start practicing, and that means keeping your momentum going, lowering the stakes, keeping yourself from overthinking, and learning what works along the way.

He’s got other helpful bits in that simple program, too: to read older work instead of new. To read a variety of sources (you can read a lot of different genres when you’re reading a new story each day!). To be on the lookout for metaphors, for things you can use in your weekly story. To write remixes by combining what you read in interesting new ways. And to commit to the practice for 1,000 days. All helpful things for a good writing practice!

I like the simplicity of the program: focus on a few good inputs a day, one good short story a week. Work at it a little each day. Finish your work and feel good about what you’ve accomplished each week. Looking at my screen time data — I could think of worse ways to spend the time!
